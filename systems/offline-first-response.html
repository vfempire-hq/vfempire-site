WHY IT HAS TO EXIST

Every recent disaster
wrote the same lesson.

Ahr valley. Valencia. The Iberian blackout. Different catastrophes, one repeated failure class: the systems meant to coordinate response assumed connectivity that the catastrophe itself removed.

F·01

Ahr 2021: the network drowned

The flood destroyed dozens of switching centres and took mobile coverage with it; alerts reached many residents near midnight, hours behind the water. More than 180 people died. Germany introduced cell-broadcast alerting only in 2023 — after.

F·02

Valencia 2024: the alert came late

The ES-Alert message reached phones roughly eight hours after flooding began in parts of the province. 223 people died. The alerting technology existed and worked — activation and coordination failed around it.

F·03

Iberia 2025: the dependency exposed

The peninsula-wide blackout took internet traffic down by 80–90%. Operators with battery-backed infrastructure stayed partly up — proving resilience is an engineering choice, made or not made before the event.

F·04

Coordination tooling assumes the cloud

Mainstream incident platforms are cloud services. When the towers are dark or the grid is down, the coordination layer vanishes precisely at its moment of purpose. Offline capability must be the foundation, not a degraded mode.

WHO IT SERVES

For the responders
the network abandons.

The municipal crisis team

The first user is the local civil-protection unit — the mayor’s crisis cell, the volunteer fire brigade, the parish flood group. They coordinate the first and worst hours, and they are the least equipped tier: no satcom trucks, no federal systems, just phones that stopped working. The system is built for them.

WHAT IT CHANGES

Coordination that degrades
gracefully — starting from zero bars.

The design baseline is total wide-area failure. Everything works locally first; connectivity, when present, makes it better rather than making it possible.

A picture without the cloud.

Incidents, resources, road states and needs tracked on a local node and device mesh — the operating picture lives in the room and the field, not in a distant data centre.

Tasking that reaches the field.

Assignments and status flowing over device-to-device links and local radio bridges — hop by hop if that is what the day allows.

Sync when the world returns.

When any link appears — one bar, one satellite terminal, one restored mast — state reconciles outward automatically. Nothing waits for connectivity; everything uses it.

THE MECHANICS

Local node, device mesh,
opportunistic everything.

The architecture is a hierarchy of survivable layers: hardened local nodes, phone-to-phone meshing, bridges to whatever radio or satellite links exist — with a data model built for disconnection and merge from the first line of code.

LAYER 1

The field layer

Responder phones running the full app offline — capture, tasking, messaging — meshing device-to-device where platform capabilities allow, storing and forwarding where they don’t.

LAYER 2

The local node

A battery-backed unit at the coordination point: local Wi-Fi island, state authority, map and data server. The crisis room works because this box works — and this box needs nothing upstream.

LAYER 3

The bridge layer

Connectors to what emergency services already trust — digital radio, amateur bridges, satellite terminals — treated as transport of opportunity, never as dependency.

LAYER 4

The sync fabric

A conflict-free data model designed for partition and merge: every node holds usable truth, and any restored link reconciles state without human deduplication.
