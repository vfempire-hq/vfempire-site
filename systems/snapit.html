WHY IT HAS TO EXIST

The camera roll is a landfill.
Sorted by nothing.

The average phone carries thousands of photos, research suggests roughly a fifth of them are duplicates and dead shots, and surveys find most people never delete any of it. Everything is technically saved. Nothing is actually findable. Four failures, one gap.

F·01

The pile only grows

Bursts, screenshots, the same sunset eleven times, WhatsApp re-saves of photos you already have. Storage research puts the junk share of a typical library at around one photo in five — and in one survey, most people admitted they never delete duplicates at all. The pile wins by default, because sorting ten years by hand is a job nobody will ever do.

F·02

The story is written inside — and ignored

Every camera writes the story into the file: the exact moment, the GPS position, the device, the burst sequence. That's enough to reconstruct events the way you remember them — the weekend away, the birthday, the site visit. Most tools use it for one thing: a timeline. The metadata deserves a filing clerk, not a scroll bar.

F·03

Messengers shred the evidence

WhatsApp and most messengers typically strip the metadata from every photo sent — the when, the where, the camera, gone. Screenshots never had any. So half a modern library is officially dated "unknown", even when the filename is shouting the date out loud. Popular organisers file these as mystery files; the answer is sitting in the name and the folder around them.

F·04

The cloud reads your photos

Free unlimited photo storage ended in 2021; two terabytes now runs about a hundred euros a year, forever. And the scanner comes with the deal: one documented case saw a father's entire Google account permanently destroyed over a toddler's medical photo — police cleared him, the account stayed dead. Google separately paid $100M to settle claims over face-grouping photos without consent. Your family archive should not live inside someone else's risk model.

WHAT IT CHANGES

From ten thousand files
to a filed archive.

The measure of a photo organiser isn't its viewer — it's whether you can find the photo you're thinking of in ten seconds, ten years later. SnapIT is judged on the finding.

Filed like a human would.

Time, place and camera cluster into events — the weekend it was, not a grid of thumbnails. Duplicates and burst near-copies fold into one keeper; screenshots get their own lane instead of polluting the memories.

Repaired, not just read.

Photos with stripped metadata get their story back — inferred from filename patterns, folder context and the photos around them. Every repaired field is marked as inferred, never silently invented. Honesty applies to data too.

Yours, on your metal.

Everything runs locally — your NAS, your desktop, your drive. No corporate scanner reads your family, no subscription meters your memories, and the output is a plain, portable folder tree no tool can hold hostage.

THE MECHANICS

Four layers,
one archive.

Built the VF way: deterministic filing driven by the file's own evidence, inference clearly marked as inference, and an output any other tool can read. SnapIT organises your library — it never takes it hostage.

LAYER 1

The reader

Every byte of embedded story extracted — capture moment, GPS position, camera and lens, burst sequence, edit history. The standard metadata that every camera writes and most software throws a timeline at.

LAYER 2

The repair bench

Stripped and mystery files get their story reconstructed — dates parsed from messenger filenames, places inferred from the folder and the photos taken around the same moment. Every repaired field carries an "inferred" mark, so the archive never lies about what it knows.

LAYER 3

The clustering

Time and place fold the library into events; perceptual matching folds duplicates, re-saves and burst shots into a single keeper with the rest tucked behind it. Screenshots, documents and work images are recognised and routed to their own lanes.

LAYER 4

The archive

The output is a clean, human-named folder tree and album set on your own storage — readable by any viewer, portable to any tool, backed up like the files they are. Your library leaves better than SnapIT found it.
